# Tell Me About a Time You Disagreed with a Coworker

**Leadership Principles:** Ownership; Have Backbone, Disagree and Commit

**Use:** [Pioneering an HGN-Based Impaired-Driving Detection Prototype](../core-stories/hgn-impaired-driving-detection.md)

## Draft answer

> I was leading a university research project, and one of the other students twice did not complete the work they had committed to. I disagreed with continuing to plan the project as though every assigned task carried the same delivery certainty. I did not want to turn the situation into a personal conflict or remove the person’s ability to contribute, but I also could not leave critical project work blocked.
>
> I reset the work plan based on criticality. I took ownership of tasks that were critical to the project or blocked other work. I assigned the other student tasks that were useful but non-blocking—work that would improve the project if completed, but would not put the outcome at risk if it was delayed. This made the delivery path more predictable while still giving the student meaningful ways to contribute when they were available.
>
> The adjustment improved the team dynamic and let us keep moving forward without repeatedly creating the same dependency risk. I learned that disagreement does not always mean arguing about an idea; sometimes it means candidly changing a working arrangement when the evidence shows the original plan is not reliable.

## What the interviewer is listening for

- You address disagreement directly and respectfully.
- You use data, customer impact, or shared goals—not ego—to make your case.
- You can commit once a decision is made, even if your preferred approach is not chosen.

## Skim notes

- Research project lead.
- Repeated missed commitments.
- Reset work plan.

## Details to add before the interview

- What conversation did you have before resetting task ownership?
- Which critical tasks did you take on, and what did they unblock?
- What useful but non-blocking tasks did the other student own?
- How did you communicate the new arrangement without making it personal?
- What did the student contribute after the plan changed?

## Likely follow-ups

- Why did you wait until the second missed commitment to change the plan?
- How did you decide which work could safely become non-blocking?
- How did you preserve the student’s opportunity to contribute?
- What would you do differently in the initial project planning?
